# Adult Women's Health Guidelines

A reference document covering health recommendations across 9 of the 12 categories tracked in the Daily Wellness Companion app (Ideas, Cycle and Bad Habits are tracked in the app but have no guideline section here). These guidelines are drawn from major health organizations including the WHO, CDC, National Academies of Medicine, National Sleep Foundation, and the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans. They are intended as general guidance — individual needs vary based on age, activity level, health conditions, pregnancy status, and other factors. Always consult a healthcare provider for personalized advice.

---

## 1. Water Intake

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine recommend that adult women consume approximately 2.7 liters (about 11.5 cups or 91 ounces) of total fluid per day from all sources, including food and beverages. Roughly 20% of daily fluid intake typically comes from food, meaning about 9 cups (2.2 liters) should come from drinks. The commonly cited "8 glasses a day" rule is a reasonable starting point but is not based on rigorous evidence — actual needs vary.

**Factors that increase water needs:** physical activity and sweating, hot or humid weather, high altitude, illness (fever, vomiting, diarrhea), pregnancy (about 10 cups/day recommended), and breastfeeding (about 12–13 cups/day).

**Signs of adequate hydration:** urine is colorless or pale yellow, you rarely feel thirsty, and you urinate roughly every 2–4 hours.

**Signs of dehydration:** dark yellow urine, headaches, dizziness, fatigue, dry mouth, and reduced cognitive performance.

**Practical tips:** sip water throughout the day rather than large amounts at once, carry a reusable water bottle, drink a glass with each meal, and note that all beverages (including tea, coffee, and milk) contribute to fluid intake. Caffeine has a mild diuretic effect but still contributes positively to hydration overall. Limit sugary drinks and be mindful that alcohol increases fluid loss.

**Over-hydration warning:** drinking excessive water (hyponatremia) is rare but potentially dangerous — it dilutes blood sodium levels. This is mainly a risk during prolonged intense exercise.

## 2. Food Intake / Nutrition

The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans emphasize eating whole, nutrient-dense foods and dramatically reducing highly processed foods. The core message is to prioritize real food: protein, dairy, vegetables, fruits, healthy fats, and whole grains.

**Daily calorie needs for women vary by age and activity level:**
- Sedentary: approximately 1,600–2,000 calories/day
- Moderately active: approximately 1,800–2,200 calories/day
- Active: approximately 2,000–2,400 calories/day

**Key nutritional recommendations:**

*Protein:* Prioritize high-quality, nutrient-dense protein from both animal sources (eggs, poultry, seafood, red meat) and plant sources (beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, tofu). Protein is essential for muscle maintenance, immune function, and hormone production.

*Fruits and vegetables:* Aim for a variety of colors and types. The general target is at least 5 servings per day. Fill roughly two-thirds of your plate with vegetables, fruits, whole grains, beans, nuts, and seeds.

*Whole grains:* 2–4 servings per day, adjusting for caloric needs. Choose fiber-rich whole grains and significantly reduce refined carbohydrates (white bread, packaged snacks, flour tortillas).

*Dairy:* Moderate amounts of milk, yogurt, and cheese for calcium, vitamin D, and protein.

*Healthy fats:* Include sources like olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish. Limit trans fats and reduce processed seed oils.

*Added sugars:* No amount of added sugars is considered part of a healthy diet. A single meal should contain no more than 10 grams of added sugars. Avoid sugary beverages.

*Sodium:* Limit to less than 2,300 mg per day.

*Alcohol:* Less is better. Women should limit to no more than 1 drink per day. Pregnant women should completely avoid alcohol.

**Nutrients of particular importance for women:** iron (especially during menstruation — RDA 18 mg/day for women 19–50), calcium (1,000–1,200 mg/day), folate (400–800 mcg/day, especially if planning pregnancy), vitamin D (600–800 IU/day), and omega-3 fatty acids.

**Meal timing:** eating at regular intervals (3 meals + snacks as needed) supports stable energy and blood sugar. Skipping meals can lead to overeating later and energy crashes.

## 3. Bathroom Breaks / Urinary Health

**Normal urinary frequency:** most women urinate 6–8 times per day during waking hours. A range of 4–10 times per day is considered normal depending on fluid intake, diet, and individual factors. Urinating more frequently than every 2 hours during the day, or waking more than once per night (nocturia), may warrant attention.

**Healthy bladder capacity:** 300–500 ml (10–17 oz). A healthy adult bladder can comfortably hold about 2 cups of urine.

**Normal bowel movements:** anywhere from 3 times per day to 3 times per week is considered normal, though daily is ideal. Consistency matters more than frequency — stool should be soft, formed, and easy to pass (types 3–4 on the Bristol Stool Scale).

**Factors affecting frequency:** fluid intake volume and type (caffeine and alcohol are bladder irritants), medications (especially diuretics), pregnancy (increased pressure on bladder), menstrual cycle, and age.

**When to seek medical attention:** burning or pain during urination, blood in urine, sudden increase in frequency, inability to fully empty the bladder, persistent nighttime urination (2+ times), foul-smelling or cloudy urine, or urinary incontinence. UTIs are common in women — symptoms include burning, urgency, and frequent small voids.

**Pelvic floor health:** Kegel exercises strengthen pelvic floor muscles and can help prevent urinary incontinence. This is especially important during pregnancy, postpartum, and with aging. Aim for 3 sets of 10–15 Kegel contractions per day.

**Bladder-friendly habits:** don't habitually hold urine for excessively long periods, don't "push" to urinate (let the bladder empty naturally), wipe front to back, and urinate after sexual intercourse to reduce UTI risk.

## 4. General Health / Energy Tracking

**Vital signs — normal ranges for adult women:**
- Resting heart rate: 60–100 bpm (fit women may be lower, around 50–70)
- Blood pressure: below 120/80 mmHg is optimal
- Body temperature: approximately 97.8–99.1°F (36.5–37.3°C)
- Respiratory rate: 12–20 breaths per minute

**Energy levels:** fluctuations throughout the day are normal. Most people experience a natural dip in alertness in the early-to-mid afternoon (the "post-lunch dip"), which is driven by circadian rhythms regardless of whether you ate lunch. Persistent low energy may indicate sleep deprivation, nutritional deficiencies (iron, B12, vitamin D), thyroid issues, depression, or chronic stress.

**Menstrual cycle effects on energy:** energy levels commonly fluctuate across the ~28-day cycle. The follicular phase (days 1–14) often brings higher energy and mood, while the luteal phase (days 15–28) can bring fatigue, irritability, and bloating. Tracking energy alongside menstrual cycle phases can reveal personal patterns.

**Common symptoms worth tracking:**
- Headaches: can indicate dehydration, tension, hormonal fluctuations, or lack of sleep
- Fatigue: persistent fatigue warrants medical evaluation
- Digestive issues: bloating, cramps, irregularity
- Pain: note location, intensity, and duration

**Preventive health screenings for women:** regular check-ups should include blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, cervical cancer screening (Pap smear), breast cancer screening (mammogram starting at age 40–50 depending on risk), bone density screening (starting at 65 or earlier with risk factors), and STI screening as appropriate.

## 5. Sleep

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the National Sleep Foundation recommend that adult women get 7–9 hours of sleep per night. Research suggests women may benefit from the higher end of this range, and some studies indicate women need approximately 15–30 extra minutes of sleep compared to men due to higher daytime brain activity and multitasking.

**Sleep quality markers:**
- Falling asleep within 15–20 minutes of getting into bed
- Sleeping through the night with no more than 1 brief awakening
- Feeling refreshed upon waking
- Spending at least 85% of time in bed actually asleep (sleep efficiency)

**Life stage considerations:**
- *Menstruation:* progesterone drops before the period can make sleep more restless. Sleep quality often dips in the late luteal phase.
- *Pregnancy:* physical discomfort, frequent urination, and anxiety can disrupt sleep. Pregnant women often need additional rest. Side sleeping (particularly left side) is recommended in later pregnancy.
- *Postpartum:* new mothers experience an average 42-minute greater decline in nightly sleep than new fathers. Short naps when possible help offset nighttime disruptions.
- *Perimenopause and menopause:* hot flashes, night sweats, and hormonal changes make sleep lighter and more fragmented. Between 30–60% of menopausal women experience insomnia.
- *Post-menopause:* sleep may shift earlier (earlier bedtime and wake time). Daytime napping becomes more common and can supplement shorter nighttime sleep.

**Sleep hygiene best practices:** maintain a consistent sleep/wake schedule (even on weekends), keep the bedroom dark, quiet, and cool (65–68°F / 18–20°C), avoid screens for at least 1 hour before bed (blue light suppresses melatonin), limit caffeine after early afternoon, avoid heavy meals within 2–3 hours of bedtime, create a calming pre-sleep routine (reading, warm bath, deep breathing), and limit alcohol before bed (it fragments sleep even if it helps you fall asleep initially).

**When to seek help:** persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep, loud snoring or gasping during sleep (possible sleep apnea), excessive daytime sleepiness despite adequate time in bed, or reliance on sleep aids.

## 6. Emotional Health / Mood

**Understanding normal emotional fluctuations:** mood naturally varies throughout the day and across the menstrual cycle. Brief periods of sadness, irritability, or anxiety in response to life events are normal. What matters is the intensity, duration, and impact on daily functioning.

**Hormonal influences on mood:**
- *Premenstrual phase:* PMS affects up to 75% of menstruating women. Symptoms include irritability, sadness, anxiety, and mood swings in the 1–2 weeks before menstruation. PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a more severe form affecting 3–8% of women.
- *Pregnancy:* mood changes are common due to hormonal shifts and life adjustment.
- *Postpartum:* "baby blues" (mild mood changes) affect up to 80% of new mothers and typically resolve within 2 weeks. Postpartum depression is more severe and persistent, affecting about 1 in 7 women.
- *Perimenopause/menopause:* fluctuating and declining estrogen can contribute to mood instability, anxiety, and depression.

**Mental health benchmarks:** the WHO identifies emotional wellbeing as a core component of health. Warning signs of clinical concern include persistent sadness or hopelessness lasting 2+ weeks, loss of interest in activities you once enjoyed, significant changes in appetite or sleep, difficulty concentrating, withdrawal from social interactions, and thoughts of self-harm.

**Emotional wellness practices:** regular physical activity (one of the strongest evidence-based mood boosters), maintaining social connections, adequate sleep, stress management techniques (mindfulness, deep breathing, journaling), limiting alcohol and caffeine, spending time outdoors, and engaging in hobbies and creative activities.

**When to seek professional help:** if mood changes interfere with work, relationships, or daily activities, or if you experience panic attacks, persistent anxiety, or intrusive thoughts. Therapy (particularly CBT) and/or medication can be highly effective.

## 7. Social Interactions / Relationships

**Why social health matters:** research consistently shows that strong social connections are associated with better physical health, longer life expectancy, improved immune function, and lower rates of depression and anxiety. Social isolation is considered a significant health risk comparable to smoking or obesity.

**General guidelines:**
- *Meaningful connections:* quality matters more than quantity. A few close, supportive relationships contribute more to wellbeing than many superficial ones.
- *Daily social interaction:* some form of meaningful social interaction most days supports emotional health. This can include conversations with family, friends, coworkers, or community members.
- *Relationship quality:* healthy relationships are characterized by mutual respect, trust, open communication, and emotional support. Tracking how interactions make you feel can help identify which relationships nourish you and which drain you.

**Journaling benefits:** writing about social interactions and emotional responses has been shown to reduce stress, improve mood, and enhance self-awareness. Even brief daily journaling (5–10 minutes) can be beneficial.

**Digital vs. in-person interaction:** in-person social interaction provides benefits that digital communication cannot fully replicate, including physical touch, nonverbal cues, and shared experiences. However, digital connections are valuable supplements, especially for maintaining long-distance relationships.

**Red flags in relationships:** patterns of control, manipulation, persistent criticism, isolation from others, or any form of abuse (physical, emotional, verbal, financial) are serious concerns. If you recognize these patterns, reaching out to a trusted person or a helpline is an important step.

## 8. Chores / Household Activity / Productivity

**Physical activity from household tasks:** household chores and daily tasks count as physical activity. Moderate-intensity household activities include vacuuming, mopping, gardening, and carrying groceries. Light-intensity activities include cooking, washing dishes, and light tidying.

**Activity guidelines (CDC and WHO):**
- 150–300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (e.g., brisk walking, active housework, cycling)
- OR 75–150 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity activity (e.g., running, fast cycling, heavy yard work)
- PLUS muscle-strengthening activities on 2+ days per week targeting all major muscle groups
- Reduce sedentary time. Any movement is better than none.

**Avoiding overexertion and injury:** use proper body mechanics when lifting (bend at knees, keep back straight), take breaks during prolonged standing tasks, alternate between sitting and standing activities, and be mindful of repetitive strain (wrists, shoulders, back).

**Mental load and task management:** research shows women disproportionately carry the "mental load" of household management — planning, organizing, remembering, and coordinating tasks beyond just completing them. This invisible labor is a significant source of stress. Strategies include externalizing tasks into written lists and shared systems, setting realistic daily expectations, delegating where possible, and recognizing that completing 60–70% of planned tasks is a successful day.

**Household cleaning frequency guidelines (general):**
- Daily: dishes, kitchen counters, quick tidying
- Weekly: vacuuming, mopping, bathroom cleaning, laundry, changing bed linens
- Monthly: deep cleaning appliances, organizing closets, dusting hard-to-reach areas
- Seasonally: window cleaning, decluttering, HVAC filter replacement

## 9. Hobbies / Leisure / Quality Time

**Why leisure matters for health:** engaging in hobbies and leisure activities is associated with lower blood pressure, lower cortisol levels, reduced depression, and improved life satisfaction. The evidence is strong that "quality time" — time spent on enjoyable, absorbing activities — is not a luxury but a health behavior.

**Recommended leisure time:** while there is no single official guideline for hobby time, research suggests that adults who engage in leisure activities for 120+ minutes per week report significantly better wellbeing. A reasonable target is 20–30 minutes per day of intentional leisure/hobby time, or 2+ hours per week.

**Types of beneficial leisure:**
- *Creative activities:* art, music, writing, crafting, and cooking stimulate different brain areas and provide a sense of accomplishment and flow.
- *Physical hobbies:* gardening, dancing, hiking, yoga, and sports combine the benefits of physical activity with enjoyment.
- *Social hobbies:* group classes, team sports, book clubs, and volunteering combine leisure with social connection.
- *Restorative activities:* reading, meditation, nature walks, and quiet reflection reduce stress and support mental health.
- *Learning:* picking up new skills (language, instrument, coding) promotes neuroplasticity and cognitive health.

**The concept of "flow":** psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's research shows that the most satisfying activities are those that induce a "flow state" — complete absorption in a task that is challenging enough to engage you but not so difficult as to cause frustration. Hobbies that produce flow states are particularly beneficial for mental health.

**Screen time considerations:** passive screen time (scrolling social media, watching TV) provides less restorative benefit than active leisure. When possible, prioritize hobbies that involve creation, movement, or social engagement over passive consumption.

**Barriers to leisure and how to address them:** guilt about "unproductive" time (reframe leisure as essential health behavior), lack of time (start with 10-minute sessions), perfectionism (the goal is enjoyment, not mastery), and exhaustion (gentle activities like reading or listening to music still count).

## General Wellness Reminders

**Annual health visits:** regular check-ups with a primary care provider are important even when feeling well. Discuss screening schedules for breast cancer, cervical cancer, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and mental health.

**Immunizations:** stay current with recommended vaccines including annual flu shot, COVID-19 boosters as recommended, Tdap every 10 years, and HPV vaccine if not previously received (recommended through age 26, may be available through 45).

**Mental health is health:** emotional wellbeing is not separate from physical health. Tracking mood, sleep, energy, social interactions, and lifestyle habits together provides a more complete picture of overall health than any single metric alone.

**Listening to your body:** the most important health guideline is paying attention to what your body tells you. Persistent changes in energy, mood, appetite, sleep, pain, or bathroom habits are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.
